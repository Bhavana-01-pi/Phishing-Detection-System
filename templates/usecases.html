Real-World Attacks

Understanding how modern phishing attacks execute data breaches helps us build better defenses.

One of the most notable political hacks involved John Podesta’s Gmail account during the 2016 election. The phishers, known as Fancy Bear, sent an email pretending to be Google, claiming a password change was required. The email linked to a malicious bit.ly URL. When clicked, it compromised his credentials, leading to the leak of thousands of emails.

BenefitMall, a payroll administration company, suffered a breach exposing employee login credentials via phishing. This exposed sensitive data including names, bank accounts, and insurance info for over 200,000 businesses.

In 2014, a state-sponsored group used spear-phishing emails containing malware to infiltrate Sony Pictures. Attackers gained network access, stole confidential data, and disabled thousands of computers, costing the company over $100 million.

Visual Examples of Phishing

Fake PayPal Notice: A classic example of creating urgency ("unusual activity") to force a quick login.

Fake Gmail Login: Visually identical to Google, but the URL is incorrect. Our visual analysis tool would catch this.

LinkedIn/Wells Fargo Phish: Attackers often mix brands (a bank email sent via LinkedIn) to confuse users.

Macro Payloads: Phishing isn't just links; it can be malicious attachments (Word/Excel) that execute code when opened.
